An air conditioner makes water every hour it runs, and the condensate line carries it away. AC leak water damage happens when that line clogs, the pan overflows or the coil freezes and melts, and the water ends up in a ceiling, closet or attic. Turn off the system at the thermostat, then call, and keep a bucket under any drip.

Why an AC unit leaks indoors

Most leaks come from a clogged condensate drain. Algae and dust build into a plug, water backs up into the pan and the pan overflows. A cracked pan, a disconnected drain line or a dirty coil that freezes and melts can also release water.

An HVAC condensate line backing up inside a closet or attic can run for days before anyone notices. The first sign is often a stain on the ceiling below the air handler or a soft spot in the closet floor.

Stain beneath the air handler

A ring on the ceiling below the unit suggests the pan overflowed.

Water around the indoor unit

A puddle at the base of the air handler means the drain is blocked.

Musty odor near vents

Moisture in the duct area can create an odor.

Unit shuts off by itself

A float switch can stop the system when the pan is full.

What to do right away

1

Turn the system off

Switch the thermostat to off so the unit stops making water.

2

Check the pan

If the pan is full, soak up water with towels or a wet vacuum.

3

Clear the line

A wet vacuum on the drain line outlet can pull the clog out. If that fails, call an HVAC technician.

4

Photograph the damage

Document the ceiling or floor before cleanup.

Dry the structure, fix the cause

An HVAC technician repairs the drain and the unit. We extract water, find hidden moisture and dry the ceiling, insulation and framing. Attic insulation under a leaking air handler is often saturated and must come out.

A leak that has run for a while can leave wet insulation and framing that dries slowly. The EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, and a long-running leak may already have mold, so we check. See our mold remediation page if growth is visible, and the ceiling repair page for stained or sagging drywall.

A float switch on the pan or drain line is an inexpensive safeguard. It shuts the system down when the pan fills, which stops a small clog from becoming a ceiling stain. An HVAC technician can add one during a service visit.

The published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration is the yardstick for how a leak like this should be dried, and it is fair to ask any company whether its crews work to it.
