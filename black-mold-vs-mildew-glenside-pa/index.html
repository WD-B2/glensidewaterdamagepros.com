Black Mold vs Mildew in Glenside, PA: What Is the Difference?

People search for black mold versus mildew because they want to know how worried to be. Here is what can and cannot be told by looking.

By the Glenside Water Damage Pros team

24/7 dispatch line
(445) 263-2078
Call (445) 263-2078

Mildew and mold are terms homeowners use for fungal growth on surfaces. Mildew is commonly used for flat, powdery or downy growth on surfaces like shower walls and window sills, while mold often describes thicker, fuzzier or darker patches. In practice, both are fungi that need moisture, and the cause and the fix are the same.

This article explains what the names mean, why color is a poor guide and what to do whichever one you have. We are a restoration company and we do not give medical advice, so questions about health belong with your physician.

What each term usually describes

Common usage of the two terms
FeatureMildew (as people use the word)Mold (as people use the word)
LookFlat, powdery, gray, white or yellowFuzzy, slimy, green, black or other colors
WhereBathrooms, windowsills, surfacesWalls, ceilings, carpet, wood, hidden cavities
DepthUsually on the surfaceCan grow into porous materials
FixDry and clean the surfaceDry, clean or remove the material

These are rough descriptions, not scientific categories. A growth that looks like mildew today can look like mold next week if the area stays wet. What matters is whether the material is hard or porous and how long it has been damp.

Why color tells you very little

Many types of mold look black, and many that look black are not harmful. Color also changes with the material and age. The EPA notes that if visible mold is present, sampling is usually unnecessary, because the next step is the same: fix the moisture and clean or remove the growth.

If you need to know exactly what is growing, an independent tester can take a sample and send it to a lab. That is usually not needed for cleanup decisions. It can help when growth is hidden, when a dispute exists or when a buyer or lender asks.

Moisture is the real question

The EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control and advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours. If a wall or ceiling stays damp, growth will come back after cleaning. Find the source, which may be a leak, condensation or a basement wall that seeps.

  • Check plumbing under sinks and behind toilets
  • Look at ceilings below bathrooms and roof lines
  • Check window sills and exterior walls for condensation
  • Look at basement walls for dampness after rain

Hard surfaces versus porous materials

Tile, glass and sealed wood can often be cleaned. Drywall, ceiling tile and carpet are porous, and the EPA notes they may need to be discarded when mold grows on them. Do not paint or caulk over growth.

If the patch covers more than 10 square feet, the EPA suggests considering a professional. For the removal process, see our mold remediation page, and for walls that stayed wet, see wall and drywall repair.

When to call someone

  • The patch is larger than about 10 square feet
  • You see growth on more than one level or in hidden spaces
  • The wet area came from sewage or contaminated water
  • The growth keeps coming back after cleaning

A simple way to think about it

Ask two questions. Is the growth on a hard surface or a porous one, and has the area been wet for days or only briefly? A patch on a tile grout line after a steamy shower is a cleaning job. A patch spreading across drywall after a roof leak is a removal job.
