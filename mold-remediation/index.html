A musty smell or dark spots on a wall

Mold is a moisture problem first and a cleaning problem second. If a wall smells musty, a ceiling shows dark spots or a leak went unattended for more than a day or two, mold may be growing where you cannot see it. Mold remediation in Glenside, PA means finding the water source, containing the area, removing contaminated material and checking that the work is finished.

The EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control and advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours. If visible mold is present, the EPA notes that sampling is usually unnecessary, so we do not start with a lab test by default. Health questions belong with your physician, and we do not give medical advice.

Mold inspection: what is checked

An inspection starts with the water. The inspector looks for the leak or humidity source, uses moisture meters and a thermal camera to find wet materials and checks the areas near plumbing, windows, the roofline and below-grade walls. The goal is a map of what is wet and what has growth, not a verdict from a smell.

Visible growth on drywall or trim

Dark, green, white or fuzzy patches on a wall or ceiling.

A musty odor with no visible source

Odor often points to hidden moisture in a wall or crawl space.

Water stains that reappear

A stain that returns after repainting means moisture is still present.

Peeling paint or warped trim

Prolonged moisture shows in the finish materials.

Testing: when it helps and when it does not

Air or surface testing can help when you cannot see the problem, when there is a dispute or when a lender or buyer asks for documentation. It does not replace fixing the water. If mold is visible, testing usually adds cost without changing the plan, which matches EPA guidance.

Testing is typically done by an independent party, so the person who tests is not the person who removes. Ask for the lab report and for how the sample locations were chosen. Our blog explains the difference between black mold and mildew in plain terms, and the same rule applies to both: fix the moisture.

How remediation is done

The work follows the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for mold remediation, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it. Crews set up containment with plastic sheeting and negative air machines that move air through HEPA filters, so spores do not spread to clean rooms. Contaminated porous materials such as drywall, carpet and ceiling tiles are removed and bagged.

Structural wood is cleaned by HEPA vacuuming and wire brushing, and affected areas are dried. The EPA advises that porous materials may need to be discarded and says not to paint or caulk over mold. A mold covering more than 10 square feet is the point where the EPA suggests considering a professional.

Clearance testing and selling or refinancing

Clearance testing is a check after remediation to show the area is back to normal conditions. It is usually done by an independent tester, and it is common when a lender, a buyer or an insurer asks for proof. We do not promise that mold will never return, because mold returns whenever moisture does.

If you are selling a home, a clearance report can answer a buyer's question about past mold. If a lender asks, request the exact documentation they need before work starts. The cause of the moisture, often a leak like those on our ceiling repair page or wall repair page, should be documented as fixed.

What to do before we arrive at a mold problem

  • Fix or shut off the water source if you can.
  • Do not disturb or dry-scrub large patches, which can spread spores.
  • Do not paint or caulk over mold, as the EPA advises.
  • Keep children and pets out of the affected room, and ask a physician about any health concern.
