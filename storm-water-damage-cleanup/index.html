Where storm water gets in

The roof is the usual entry point, through lifted shingles, damaged flashing or a vent boot. Windows and doors leak when wind drives rain against failed seals. Walls can take water through damaged siding, and chimneys leak at the flashing.

A roofer handles the roof. We handle the water inside. Wet insulation is removed, the ceiling and wall cavities are dried and drywall that cannot be saved is replaced. Hidden moisture is mapped with a thermal camera and moisture meters.

Beware of a "free storm inspection" pitch

The Pennsylvania Insurance Department warns about storm-chasing scams. Contractors doing more than $5,000 of work a year in Pennsylvania must register with the Attorney General, so ask any contractor for proof before paying.

Documenting storm damage

Photograph the roof from the ground, the ceiling stains, the wet insulation if you can see it and any damaged windows or siding. Write down the date and time the storm hit and when you first noticed water inside. A weather record from the National Weather Service can back up the date.

Gutters, downspouts and drainage

Storm water often enters because the exterior could not shed it fast enough. A clogged gutter spills water down a wall, and a downspout that ends beside the foundation feeds the basement. Fixing these is outside restoration, but we point out what we see.

Safety steps before help arrives

  • Stay off the roof, especially when it is wet or windy.
  • Keep clear of downed lines and call PECO or 911 about them.
  • Do not use a ceiling fixture that is dripping.
  • Cover openings with a tarp only if you can do it safely from the ground or inside.

According to the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration, storm water that enters from outside is handled as potentially contaminated, and the Pennsylvania Insurance Department guidelines cover temporary repairs.
