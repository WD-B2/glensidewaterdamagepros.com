Water Damage Categories and Classes Explained for Glenside, PA Homes

Categories describe the water. Classes describe the building. Here is how each is used and why it matters for your estimate.

By the Glenside Water Damage Pros team

24/7 dispatch line
(445) 263-2078
Call (445) 263-2078

If an estimate says category 2, class 3, the words may sound like code. They come from the industry standard ANSI/IICRC S500, and they are used to decide how a water loss is handled. Once you know what they mean, you can read an estimate and ask better questions.

This guide explains both in plain language. It is general information, and every loss needs an on-site assessment.

Categories describe the water source

The three categories
CategoryMeaningExampleUsual result
Category 1Water from a clean sourceBurst supply line, sink overflowOften dried
Category 2Significant contaminationWasher or dishwasher dischargeOften cleaned, porous items may go
Category 3Grossly contaminatedSewage, floodwaterPorous materials discarded

Clean water can move down the categories over time, since time and contact with materials add contamination. A clean leak left for days may no longer be category 1.

Classes describe how wet the building is

The four classes
ClassMeaningExample
Class 1Least water, little absorbedPart of a room, low-absorption materials
Class 2Whole room, carpet and pad wetWet carpet and walls up to a short height
Class 3Water from above, saturated materialsCeiling, walls, insulation and carpet
Class 4Specialty drying, dense materialsHardwood, plaster, concrete

Classes help crews pick equipment and estimate drying time. Class 4 jobs use specialty drying methods, and they take longer.

Why it matters for the estimate

  • Category 3 means more removal and disposal
  • Higher classes mean more equipment and days
  • A category change over time can change the plan
  • Documentation of both supports an insurance claim

If your estimate lists a category and class, ask how they were decided. A good crew can show where they measured and what they found.

What you can do

Stop the source, keep people away from contaminated water and photograph the damage. The Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises photographing or recording before cleanup. For sewage, see sewage backup cleanup, and for floodwater, see flood damage cleanup.

Category 1 in more detail

Category 1 water comes from a source that does not pose a substantial risk when first released. A broken supply line, a tub overflow and rainwater coming through a clean roof are examples. The category is not permanent, and clean water that sits can pick up bacteria and soil.

Category 2 in more detail

Category 2 water, once called grey water, has some contamination and may cause discomfort if people are exposed to it. Washing machine discharge, dishwasher overflow and toilet overflow with urine only are common sources. Carpet and pad that sat in it are often replaced, and hard surfaces are cleaned.

Category 3 in more detail

Category 3 water is grossly contaminated. Sewage, floodwater from rivers and streams and water from a toilet that carries waste are in this group. Porous materials are discarded, and the crew wears protective gear. The EPA recommends using professionals for sewage or contaminated water.

Why a loss can be reclassified

Time and temperature matter. A category 1 loss that was left for several days may be reclassified after assessment, since bacteria grow in warm, wet materials. That is why prompt response often changes both the category and the cost.

What to do before you know the category

  • Treat unknown water as contaminated until a crew checks it.
  • Keep children and pets out of the water.
  • Wear boots and gloves if you must enter.
  • Do not use a household vacuum on standing water.

How a crew decides the category on site

The crew asks where the water came from, how long it has been there and what it touched. They look at the water, smell it and check whether it has crossed a floor into other rooms. They also check what the water passed through, since a clean line that ran through a dirty crawl space is not what it was when it left the pipe.

The decision is written down with a reason. If you disagree, ask for the reason. A good crew explains the assessment, and you can ask your insurer to review it.

Safety by category

  • Category 1: stop the source, lift items, keep power off to wet areas
  • Category 2: wear boots and gloves, keep children and pets out of the water
  • Category 3: stay out of the water, use professionals, and call PECO or 911 for electrical risk

Categories in Glenside

Glenside is served by two townships, and sewer arrangements differ. Abington has its own wastewater plant for the western third of the township, and areas east of Edge Hill Road flow to the Philadelphia Water Department's Pennypack interceptor.

If water comes up a drain, ask the township which system serves your street, and look at our page for Wyncote and Flourtown for their sewer notes.
