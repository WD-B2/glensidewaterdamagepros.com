How Fast Does Mold Grow After Water Damage in Glenside, PA?

The first two days after a leak decide most of the mold risk. Here is what the EPA says and how to use it.

By the Glenside Water Damage Pros team

24/7 dispatch line
(445) 263-2078
Call (445) 263-2078

Mold needs moisture, and water damage supplies it. The EPA states that it is important to dry water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. It also says that if wet or damp materials are dried within that window, mold will in most cases not grow.

This guide explains what that window means in practice. We do not give medical advice, and no source we cite says how fast any specific mold appears in your house.

What the 24 to 48 hour window means

The window is the time you have to dry materials before conditions favor growth. It is not a countdown to a visible patch. Mold may start sooner or later depending on the material, the temperature and the humidity.

Why timing matters
Time since the leakWhat to doRisk
Under 24 hoursStop the source, extract, start dryingLowest
24 to 48 hoursDry fully, check hidden areasRising
Over 48 hoursInspect for growth, consider removal of porous materialsHigher
Days or weeksInspect and test as neededGrowth may already be present

What speeds growth

  • Wet porous materials such as drywall, carpet and ceiling tile
  • Warm, humid, closed-up rooms
  • Hidden moisture inside wall cavities
  • Water that sat on materials rather than draining away

Dense materials such as concrete and sealed tile are less prone to growth, while paper-faced drywall gives mold something to feed on.

How to beat the clock

  1. Stop the source of water.
  2. Remove standing water.
  3. Run dehumidifiers and fans, and open windows only if outdoor air is drier.
  4. Lift wet carpet and pad if they cannot be dried quickly.
  5. Check walls with a moisture meter or call a crew to map them.

Our water extraction page and structural drying page cover the first two steps in detail.

If it has been longer than 48 hours

Do not panic. Inspect for visible growth and smell, and have the area measured. The EPA says that if visible mold is present, sampling is usually unnecessary. It also says porous materials may need to be discarded and not to paint or caulk over mold.

For a patch larger than 10 square feet, the EPA suggests considering a professional. See our mold remediation page for how that process runs. Health questions belong with a physician.

What mold needs

Mold needs a food source, a suitable temperature and moisture. Houses supply the first two all the time, since wood, paper and dust are plentiful. Water supplies the third, which is why the EPA calls moisture control the key.

Where mold hides after a leak

  • Behind baseboards and wallpaper
  • Inside wall cavities and under insulation
  • Under carpet pad and sheet vinyl
  • On the back of drywall and ceiling tiles

A 48 hour action plan

  1. Hour 0 to 2: stop the source, photograph, call for help.
  2. Hour 2 to 12: extract water, remove soaked pad and start dehumidifiers.
  3. Hour 12 to 24: check walls with a meter, open wet cavities if needed.
  4. Hour 24 to 48: keep drying, confirm readings are falling and plan repairs.

The 24 to 48 hour guideline comes from the EPA, and the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for mold remediation is what applies if growth is already present.

Why drywall and carpet are the first concern

Paper-faced drywall, ceiling tile and carpet pad are porous, which means they take water in and give it up slowly. Those are the materials the EPA says may need to be discarded if they stay wet. A solid wood joist that got wet and dried within days is a different case, since wood can recover.

When a crew finds wet drywall, the question is how long it has been wet and whether it can be dried in place. A reading, a smell and the water category give the answer. If in doubt, removing a foot of drywall is cheaper than treating mold behind it.

What a quick inspection involves

A crew checks the walls near the leak with a moisture meter and looks at the baseboards and the back of cabinets. A small hole in a wall lets a camera see the cavity. The goal is to know where the water is, so drying can be aimed at the right places.

Mold timing in Glenside homes

Humid summers in Montgomery County mean closed-up basements stay damp, and the clock runs faster there. Abington Township's stormwater plan names the Keswick neighborhood as a place that has experienced flooding, so check lower levels after storms.

Neighbors in Ambler and Wyncote can use the same 24 to 48 hour guide.
